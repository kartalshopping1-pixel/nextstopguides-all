Osaka has long been called "the nation's kitchen", and locals have a word, *kuidaore*, that roughly means "eat until you drop". The city's food is less formal than Kyoto's and less polished than Tokyo's: think street snacks, sizzling griddles, standing bars and noisy counters. This guide covers what to eat, where to find it and how ordering works, so you can walk in anywhere with confidence.

## The must-try Osaka dishes

### Takoyaki

Balls of batter filled with pieces of octopus, cooked in special moulded pans and topped with sauce, mayonnaise, bonito flakes and seaweed powder. You will find stands all over Dotonbori and Namba. They come straight off the griddle and are extremely hot inside — wait a minute before you bite.

### Okonomiyaki

A savoury pancake of batter, shredded cabbage and your choice of fillings such as pork, seafood or cheese, cooked on a hot plate. Osaka-style okonomiyaki mixes everything together before cooking (Hiroshima-style layers the ingredients with noodles). In many restaurants the staff cook it for you; in others you cook it yourself at your table's griddle.

### Kushikatsu

Skewers of meat, seafood and vegetables, breaded and deep-fried. The Shinsekai district is famous for it. There is one rule everyone learns quickly: the shared sauce pot is for dipping once only — **no double dipping**. Use the free cabbage leaves to scoop extra sauce if you need it.

### Other favourites

- **Kitsune udon:** thick noodles in light broth topped with sweet fried tofu, an Osaka classic.
- **Butaman:** steamed pork buns; the local chain 551 Horai is famous for them and sells them in stations and department stores.
- **Horumon:** grilled offal, popular in izakaya and yakiniku restaurants.
- **Kaiten sushi:** conveyor-belt sushi is an easy, affordable introduction to sushi, with ordering often done on a tablet.
- **Cheesecake and sweets:** jiggly Japanese-style cheesecake and taiyaki (fish-shaped cakes filled with red bean paste) are common snacks.

## Where to eat

### Dotonbori and Namba

The neon-lit canal of Dotonbori, with its giant signs and the famous running-man billboard, is Osaka's food playground. It is touristy and busy, but fun at night, and the takoyaki and okonomiyaki here are genuinely good. Wander into the side streets such as Hozenji Yokocho, a quiet stone-paved lane with small restaurants and a moss-covered statue.

### Shinsekai

An old-fashioned district around the Tsutenkaku tower, packed with kushikatsu restaurants and retro signs. It is especially atmospheric in the evening.

### Kuromon Market

A covered market with seafood, fruit, grilled skewers and Kobe-style beef stalls. It is convenient for grazing, but it has become very tourist-oriented and prices are higher than in local supermarkets. Go for the experience rather than for bargains.

### Tenjinbashi-suji shopping street

One of Japan's longest covered shopping streets, stretching for over two kilometres north of the centre. It is less touristy than Dotonbori and full of small restaurants, bakeries and croquette shops.

### Umeda

The area around Osaka and Umeda stations has huge department stores with basement food halls (depachika) and restaurant floors, plus the old-school izakaya alleys under and around the station.

## How ordering works

Japanese restaurants can seem confusing the first time, but most follow a few simple patterns.

1. **Ticket machines (shokkenki).** Many ramen and noodle shops have a vending machine at the entrance. You choose your dish, pay (often cash only), and hand the ticket to the staff. Photos usually help; if not, a translation app's camera mode works well.
2. **Tablets and QR codes.** Increasingly, you order on a tablet or by scanning a QR code at your table. Many have an English option.
3. **Picture menus and plastic food.** Plastic food displays in the window show what each dish looks like. Pointing is completely acceptable.
4. **Paying.** In many restaurants you take the bill to the cashier at the exit instead of paying at the table.

> **Tip:** Many izakaya charge a small cover charge (often called otoshi), which comes with a small appetiser you did not order. It is normal, not a scam.

## Etiquette in a nutshell

- **No tipping.** Service is included in the culture, and leaving money can confuse staff.
- **Say "itadakimasu" before eating and "gochisousama deshita" when leaving.** Staff appreciate the effort.
- **Slurping noodles is fine.** It is considered normal and even a sign you are enjoying them.
- **Chopsticks:** do not stick them upright in rice or pass food from chopstick to chopstick; both are associated with funerals.
- **Queues are normal.** Popular places often have a line outside; join it and wait your turn.
- **Carry cash.** Small restaurants and street stalls may not take cards.

## Eating on a budget

Osaka is one of Japan's most affordable food cities. Convenience stores sell surprisingly good onigiri, sandwiches and hot snacks; supermarkets discount bento boxes in the evening; and standing bars (tachinomi) serve drinks and small plates at low prices. Lunch sets (teishoku) in local restaurants are usually much cheaper than dinner at the same place.
