In Japan you will use your phone constantly: for train routes, maps, translations, restaurant searches and digital tickets. Free Wi-Fi exists in many stations, cafés and hotels, but it is not reliable enough to depend on when you are out exploring. So before you fly, you need to choose how you will get mobile data. The three main options are an eSIM, a pocket Wi-Fi router, or a physical SIM card — plus your home carrier's roaming. Here is how they compare.

## Option 1: eSIM

An eSIM is a digital SIM card you install on your phone by scanning a QR code or through an app. You can buy it online before your trip and it activates when you connect to a Japanese network.

**Advantages**

- **Ready when you land.** Install it at home, switch it on after landing and you are online before passport control.
- **Nothing to carry, collect or return.** No extra device to charge or lose.
- **Keep your home number active.** On phones with dual SIM support, you can keep your regular SIM for calls and texts (useful for bank verification codes) while using the eSIM for data.
- **Flexible plans.** You can choose a data amount and duration that match your trip and often top up later if you need more.

**Disadvantages**

- **Your phone must support eSIM and be carrier-unlocked.** Most recent iPhones and many recent Android phones do, but check your phone's settings or your manufacturer's website before buying.
- **Usually data only.** Most travel eSIMs do not include a Japanese phone number for calls.
- **One device.** Sharing data with others depends on whether your plan allows tethering (personal hotspot).

## Option 2: Pocket Wi-Fi

A pocket Wi-Fi is a small portable router you rent for your trip. It connects to a Japanese mobile network and creates a Wi-Fi hotspot for your devices.

**Advantages**

- **Works with any phone**, including older or locked phones without eSIM support.
- **Shareable.** Several people and devices can connect at once — convenient for couples, families and people travelling with laptops or tablets.
- **Often generous data allowances**, which suit heavy users.

**Disadvantages**

- **Another device to carry and charge.** If the battery dies in the afternoon, everyone loses data — a [power bank](packing:power-bank) becomes essential.
- **You have to stay together.** If your group splits up, only the person with the router stays connected.
- **Pick-up and return.** You collect it at an airport counter or have it delivered to your hotel, and must return it at the end (usually at the airport or by post). Losing or damaging it can be expensive.

## Option 3: Physical SIM card

Prepaid data SIM cards are sold at airports, electronics stores and online. They work like an eSIM but require swapping the SIM card in your phone, which means removing your home SIM (keep it somewhere safe). They are mainly useful if your phone is unlocked but does not support eSIM.

## What about roaming?

Some home carriers offer daily travel packages for Japan. They are the simplest option — no setup at all — and let you keep your number. However, they can be significantly more expensive for longer trips, and some include slow speeds after a small daily allowance. Check your carrier's current offer and compare it with the cost of an eSIM for the same number of days.

## Which option should you choose?

- **Solo traveller or couple with modern phones:** an eSIM is usually the easiest and most flexible choice.
- **Family or group that stays together, or someone with a phone that does not support eSIM:** a pocket Wi-Fi can make more sense.
- **Short business trip, want zero setup:** your carrier's roaming package might be worth the premium.
- **Heavy users who stream or work online:** compare the data allowances carefully; a larger eSIM plan or a pocket Wi-Fi with a high limit may be better.

## How much data do you need?

It depends heavily on how you use your phone, so we will not guess a number for you. As a rule of thumb, maps, messaging and browsing use relatively little; video calls, streaming and uploading lots of photos and videos use a lot. A sensible approach is to choose a mid-sized plan, check your usage after the first couple of days and top up if needed. Using hotel Wi-Fi for backups and large uploads helps a lot.

## Setting up an eSIM: step by step

1. **Check compatibility.** Make sure your phone supports eSIM and is unlocked.
2. **Buy the eSIM before you travel** and install it at home while you have stable Wi-Fi. Do not delete it after installation — many eSIMs cannot be reinstalled.
3. **Label it** (for example "Japan data") and keep your home line as the default for calls and texts if you want.
4. **On landing, switch data to the eSIM** and turn on data roaming for that line (this is normal for travel eSIMs and does not use your home carrier's roaming).
5. **Turn off data roaming on your home line** to avoid unexpected charges.

> **Tip:** Download offline maps of the areas you will visit and save your hotel addresses in Japanese as a backup, in case you ever lose signal or your battery runs low.

One widely used option is [Airalo](partner:airalo), which sells Japan eSIMs you can install before you leave and top up in its app. Whichever provider you choose, compare data amount, validity period and whether hotspot sharing is allowed.

## Other connectivity tips

- **Power is the real limit.** Navigation drains batteries quickly; carry a [power bank](packing:power-bank) and the right [plug adapter](packing:adapter).
- **Translation apps with camera mode** are invaluable for menus and signs, and many work partly offline if you download the language pack.
- **Digital tickets:** keep screenshots of QR codes and booking confirmations in case you are offline at the gate.
- **Public Wi-Fi:** avoid logging into banking or sensitive accounts on open networks.
