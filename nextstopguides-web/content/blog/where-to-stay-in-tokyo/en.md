Tokyo is huge, but for a first visit the choice of where to stay comes down to a handful of neighborhoods. The right one depends less on "which area is best" and more on three practical questions: how you arrive, where you will spend most of your days, and what kind of evenings you enjoy. This guide compares the main options honestly, including their downsides.

## The one rule that matters most: stay near a good station

In Tokyo you will spend a lot of time on trains, so your hotel's distance to a station matters more than the neighborhood's name. Ideally, choose a hotel within a five-to-ten-minute walk of a station on the JR Yamanote Line (the loop line connecting most central districts) or on a subway line that runs through the areas you want to see. Every extra transfer adds up over several days.

Also think about your airport:

- **Narita Airport** is linked by the Narita Express to Tokyo Station, Shinagawa, Shibuya, Shinjuku and Ikebukuro, and by the Keisei Skyliner to Nippori and Ueno.
- **Haneda Airport** is much closer to the city, with the Keikyu Line to Shinagawa and the Tokyo Monorail to Hamamatsucho, plus direct airport buses to many hotels.

If you land late at night with luggage, a hotel close to these connections — or a pre-booked transfer with [Welcome Pickups](partner:welcomepickups) — makes the first evening much easier.

## Shinjuku: best all-round base for most first-timers

Shinjuku is one of the busiest transport hubs in the world, with JR lines, several subway lines and private railways (including the Odakyu Line to Hakone). From here you can reach almost anywhere without many changes.

**Good for:** easy transport, a huge choice of hotels at every budget, late-night food and bars, day trips to Hakone.

**Keep in mind:** the station itself is a maze, so choose a hotel near the exit you will actually use. The Kabukicho entertainment district north-east of the station is lively and generally safe, but not everyone likes the atmosphere late at night. The west side is calmer and more business-like.

## Shibuya: young, busy and very convenient

Shibuya is famous for its scramble crossing and is packed with shops, cafés and rooftop views. It sits on the Yamanote Line and several subway lines, and it is close to Harajuku, Omotesando and Ebisu.

**Good for:** shopping, nightlife, first-time "big city" energy, easy access to the west side of Tokyo.

**Keep in mind:** it is crowded almost all the time, and hotel prices can be higher than in comparable areas. Rooms near the station are often small.

## Tokyo Station and Ginza: central and practical

The area around Tokyo Station (Marunouchi and Nihonbashi) and nearby Ginza is polished, central and very well connected. Most Shinkansen departures, including trains to Kyoto and Osaka, leave from Tokyo Station.

**Good for:** short stays, travellers heading on by Shinkansen, business-hotel comfort, upscale shopping and dining in Ginza.

**Keep in mind:** the business district can feel quiet in the evenings and at weekends, and the hotel choice skews towards mid-range and luxury.

## Asakusa: traditional atmosphere, lower prices

Asakusa is home to Senso-ji, Tokyo's oldest temple, and has a more old-fashioned, low-rise feel than the rest of the city. Many guesthouses and good-value hotels are here, and the Asakusa subway line connects directly towards both Haneda and Narita via through-services.

**Good for:** a traditional atmosphere, early-morning temple visits before the crowds, budget and mid-range stays, travellers who enjoy quieter evenings.

**Keep in mind:** it is further from the west side of Tokyo (Shinjuku, Shibuya, Harajuku), so those days will involve longer rides. Much of the area goes quiet fairly early at night.

## Ueno: museums, parks and Narita access

Ueno combines a large park with several major museums, a zoo and the busy Ameya-Yokocho market street. With the Keisei Skyliner from Narita arriving here, it is a practical first or last night.

**Good for:** Narita arrivals and departures, museum lovers, budget-friendly hotels, easy access to Asakusa and Akihabara.

**Keep in mind:** it has less nightlife and fewer "wow" streets than Shinjuku or Shibuya, and some blocks near the station are a bit rough around the edges.

## Ikebukuro: good value on the Yamanote Line

Ikebukuro is a major hub on the north-west of the Yamanote Line with department stores, anime and game shops and plenty of restaurants. It is often slightly cheaper than Shinjuku for similar hotels.

**Good for:** value, families (large shopping complexes, an aquarium), good transport without Shinjuku-level crowds.

**Keep in mind:** it is less central for the east side of the city and not a sightseeing area in itself.

## Roppongi and Akasaka: art, views and nightlife

Roppongi mixes international nightlife with serious art museums and observation decks, while neighboring Akasaka is quieter and hotel-heavy.

**Good for:** art lovers, higher-end hotels, evenings out.

**Keep in mind:** Roppongi is not on the Yamanote Line, so some journeys need an extra change, and its late-night strip is not to everyone's taste.

## How to choose in two minutes

1. **First trip, not sure?** Shinjuku or near Tokyo Station.
2. **Nightlife and shopping?** Shibuya or Shinjuku.
3. **Traditional feel and better value?** Asakusa or Ueno.
4. **Arriving at Narita?** Consider Ueno or near Tokyo Station for the first night.
5. **Continuing by Shinkansen early in the morning?** Tokyo Station or Shinagawa.

It is also perfectly fine to split your stay: for example two nights in Shinjuku for the west side and Hakone access, then two nights near Tokyo Station before heading to Kyoto.

## Booking tips

- Check the room size in square metres; many Tokyo rooms are compact, and "double" beds can be narrower than you are used to.
- Read the check-in time carefully — it is often 3 pm — and ask whether you can leave luggage earlier.
- Look for free cancellation where possible, so you can adjust once your route is fixed. You can compare hotels on [Klook](partner:hotels).
- Prices rise sharply during cherry blossom season, Golden Week, Obon and around New Year, so book early for those dates. See [the best time to visit Japan](post:best-time-to-visit-japan).
