Packing for Japan is mostly about two things: travelling light enough for stations, stairs and small hotel rooms, and bringing a few items that make long walking days much easier. This list focuses on what is genuinely useful in Japan rather than a generic travel checklist. You can find the specific products we suggest on our [travel packing list](packing:) page.

## Documents and money

- **Passport** — keep it with you when shopping, as tax-free purchases require the physical passport.
- **Printed or offline copies** of hotel bookings, flight details and your itinerary.
- **Travel insurance details**, including the emergency phone number.
- **Two payment cards from different networks** (for example Visa and Mastercard), in case one is not accepted or gets blocked.
- **Some yen in cash** for your arrival day; you can withdraw more at convenience store ATMs.
- **A small coin purse.** Japan uses many coins, including 500-yen coins, and they pile up quickly.

## Electronics and power

- **Plug adapter.** Japan uses Type A sockets (two flat pins) and 100 V electricity. Most phone and laptop chargers accept 100–240 V and only need an [adapter](packing:adapter). Check the label before plugging in anything else.
- **Power bank.** Maps and translation apps drain batteries fast. A [10,000 mAh power bank](packing:power-bank) is a good size, but remember that airlines require power banks to travel in your cabin bag, not in checked luggage, and some have additional rules — check with your airline.
- **Charging cables** for every device, and ideally a charger with several USB ports so you only need one socket.
- **Mobile data.** Set up an eSIM or book a pocket Wi-Fi before you fly — see [Japan eSIM vs pocket Wi-Fi](post:japan-esim-vs-pocket-wifi). If your phone supports it, an eSIM from [Airalo](partner:airalo) can be installed at home.

> **Tip:** Hair dryers, straighteners and other heating appliances from 220–240 V countries often do not work properly on Japan's 100 V. Most hotels provide a hair dryer, so it is usually best to leave yours at home.

## Clothes and shoes

- **Comfortable, broken-in walking shoes.** This is the single most important item. Japanese city days easily involve many kilometres of walking, plus stairs at every station.
- **Shoes that are easy to slip on and off.** You will remove them in temples, ryokan, some restaurants and fitting rooms.
- **Socks in good condition** — you will be showing them more often than you expect.
- **Layers.** Trains, shops and museums can be strongly air-conditioned in summer and warm in winter, so layers work better than one heavy item.
- **Modest options** for visiting temples and shrines; there is no strict dress code at most sites, but very revealing clothes can feel out of place.
- **Laundry plan.** Many hotels have coin laundry, so you can pack for about a week even on a longer trip. For a neat look after a day in the suitcase, a [mini travel steam iron](packing:steam-iron) helps — check that it supports 100 V or is dual-voltage.

## For long walking days

- **A secure day bag.** A crossbody bag with zipped compartments keeps your passport, cards and IC card close in busy stations. An [anti-theft day bag](packing:day-bag) with RFID-blocking pockets is a good choice.
- **Blister bandages.** Hydrocolloid [blister plasters](packing:blister) are small, light and can save a whole day of sightseeing.
- **Compact umbrella.** Rain is common, especially in the rainy season from roughly June to July on the main island. A [windproof travel umbrella](packing:umbrella) fits in any bag; convenience stores also sell cheap umbrellas if you get caught out.
- **Small towel or handkerchief.** Many public restrooms do not have paper towels or hand dryers, which is why many people in Japan carry a small hand towel.
- **Reusable water bottle.** Vending machines are everywhere, but a bottle helps in summer heat.
- **A small bag for rubbish.** Public bins are rare, so you will often carry your rubbish until you find one.

## Toiletries and health

- **Travel-size toiletries** in leak-proof bottles; for carry-on luggage on international flights, liquids are generally limited to containers of 100 ml or less. A set of [silicone travel bottles](packing:bottles) makes this easy.
- **Personal medication** in its original packaging, with a copy of your prescription. Japan has strict rules on some medicines, including certain over-the-counter cold and allergy medicines that are legal elsewhere. Check the official guidance before you travel if you take any regular medication.
- **Sunscreen and sunglasses** in summer, and lip balm and hand cream in winter.
- **Basic first-aid items**: painkillers you know, plasters and anything you rely on.

## Luggage and organisation

- **A suitcase you can lift up stairs.** Medium-sized is ideal. Very large bags are hard to manage on trains, and on some Shinkansen lines large luggage needs a special seat reservation.
- **Packing cubes.** When you move between hotels every two or three nights, [packing cubes](packing:packing-cubes) make packing and unpacking much faster.
- **A foldable tote bag** for shopping and souvenirs — Japan charges for plastic bags in many shops.
- **Luggage forwarding** instead of dragging everything: hotels and convenience stores can send your suitcase ahead to your next hotel.

## Nice-to-haves

- **Portable coffee maker** for early starts before cafés open — a rechargeable [portable espresso maker](packing:espresso) is a small luxury on long trips.
- **Earplugs and an eye mask** for light sleepers in city hotels.
- **A pen**, for arrival forms and temple stamp books (goshuincho), which make a lovely souvenir.

## What to leave at home

- Too many shoes — one comfortable pair plus one lighter pair is enough.
- Heavy guidebooks — use offline maps and a printable itinerary instead.
- Your full-size toiletries — Japanese hotels usually provide shampoo, conditioner, body soap and often a toothbrush.
- Anything you would be unhappy to carry up three flights of station stairs.

## Seasonal notes

What you need changes a lot by season: light, breathable clothes and sun protection in the hot and humid summer; a warm coat, hat and gloves for winter, especially in northern Japan; and layers for spring and autumn, when mornings and evenings are cool. See [the best time to visit Japan](post:best-time-to-visit-japan) for a season-by-season overview.
